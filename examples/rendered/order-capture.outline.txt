[Heading1] Contents
[Heading1] 1. Summary
[-] Order Capture API experienced an 11% HTTP 5xx error rate on order submissions from 09:12 to 10:26 AEST on 3 September 2026. Change CHG-88213, deployed at 09:04, raised the service's downstream concurrency limit from 50 to 200 without raising the database connection pool (fixed at 60), and the pool was exhausted under normal Wednesday-morning load. Customer care reported failures at 09:21, an alert fired at 09:27, pool exhaustion was confirmed as the cause at 10:05, and a rollback restored normal error rates at 10:26. Approximately 4,200 order submissions from around 3,100 customers failed during the window and 612 queued orders were delivered up to 40 minutes late. No orders were lost or duplicated. The rollback is a permanent fix for this change; the underlying gaps are addressed by CA-1 to CA-4.

[Heading1] 4. Customer impact
[-] Question
[-] Answer
[-] Who was affected (segments, regions, channels, partners)?
[-] Consumer and small-business customers submitting orders through the web and app channels in all regions.
[-] What did they experience?
[-] 'Something went wrong, please try again' on submit; retries also failed in most cases while the pool was exhausted.
[-] How many were affected? How do we know?
[-] 4,180 failed submissions from 3,090 distinct customer IDs between 09:12 and 10:26, about 11% of submissions in the window. Counted by: Log query PIR-0412-failed over the gateway access logs, de-duplicated by customer ID.
[-] For how long?
[-] 74 minutes of failed submissions (09:12 to 10:26), plus 21 minutes of delayed delivery for queued orders (to 10:47).
[-] Business metrics affected
[-] An estimated 1,900 orders were not re-attempted by 17:00 compared with the normal re-attempt rate, roughly $86k of order value at risk; 612 orders delivered 20 to 40 minutes late; support contacts 3.4 times the hourly norm between 09:30 and 11:00.
[-] Commitments breached (SLA, SLO, regulatory)?
[-] Monthly order-submit availability SLO (99.9%) consumed 58% of its error budget in one event. No contractual SLA breached.
[-] Customers / partners contacted?
[-] Two enterprise partners contacted by the account team at 11:30 on 3 Sep; both confirmed impact matched our estimate.
[-] What was not affected?
[-] Retail-store channel (separate order path); order status lookups; payments.
[-] Any data loss, duplication or corruption?
[-] No orders lost or duplicated, verified by reconciling the order queue against the orders table at 11:05.
[Heading1] 5. Incident response and post-incident analysis
[Heading2] 5.1 What went well
[ListBullet] Once pool exhaustion was confirmed, the decision to roll back rather than tune was made in 9 minutes and executed in 12, because the rollback runbook was current and had been rehearsed in August.
[ListBullet] Customer care escalated the first reports to the on-call channel within 6 minutes of noticing them.
[Heading2] 5.2 What we missed
[-] Area
[-] What we missed or what slowed us down
[-] Effect on the incident
[-] Detection (time to detect)
[-] The only alert on this path requires 5% errors sustained for 10 minutes, so it fired 15 minutes after impact start and 6 minutes after customer care had already noticed.
[-] The first 9 minutes of the 74-minute impact window had no signal at all, and the next 6 relied on customer care rather than monitoring.
[-] Diagnosis (time to know)
[-] Pool saturation is not on the service dashboard; it was found by an ad-hoc query after the first hypothesis was rejected.
[-] Around 25 of the 44 minutes of time to know.
[-] Remediation (time to remediate)
[-] Rollback required a manual decision and a manual pipeline trigger; no automated rollback exists for this service.
[-] About 12 of the 21 minutes of time to remediate were mechanical steps a deploy guard could have taken.
[-] Prevention
[-] CHG-88213 was classed as 'config only, low risk' and so required neither a load test nor a canary stage.
[-] The interaction between concurrency and pool size was never exercised before production.

[Heading1] 6. Five Whys (root cause analysis)
[Heading2] 6.1 Chain A: the failure (CH-1)
[-] Problem statement: POST /orders returned 5xx for 11% of requests from 09:12.
[-] Why?
[-] Because (answer, with evidence)
[-] Why 1: Why did requests fail?
[-] Requests timed out waiting for a database connection; pool in-use was 60 of 60 from 09:11. Evidence: FIG-2 panel A
[-] Why 2: Why was the pool exhausted?
[-] CHG-88213 raised downstream concurrency from 50 to 200, so up to 200 in-flight requests competed for 60 connections. Evidence: CHG-88213 diff; deploy log
[-] Why 3: Why could concurrency be raised without the pool?
[-] The two settings are tuned independently; nothing documents or enforces that pool size must scale with concurrency. Evidence: service configuration reference, no cross-reference between the two keys
[-] Why 4: Why was the interaction not caught before production?
[-] The change was not load-tested, so the interaction was never exercised. Evidence: CHG-88213 record shows no load-test stage
[-] Why 5: Why was it not load-tested?
[-] Our change policy classes configuration-only changes as low risk and exempts them from pre-production load testing and canary release. Evidence: change policy section 4.2
[-] Root cause RC-A: Change policy treats capacity-affecting configuration as low risk, so such changes reach production without load testing or a canary.
[Heading2] 6.2 Chain B: the detection gap (CH-2)
[-] Problem statement: The first alert fired 15 minutes after impact start, after a customer-care report.
[-] Why?
[-] Because (answer, with evidence)
[-] Why 1: Why did the alert take 15 minutes?
[-] The alert requires 5% errors sustained for 10 minutes. Evidence: alert A-2231 definition
[-] Why 2: Why is the window 10 minutes?
[-] It was widened from 2 to 10 minutes in May 2026 after false pages during a batch job. Evidence: alert change history, May 2026
[-] Why 3: Why did it need de-tuning?
[-] The alert is on an infrastructure symptom (5xx) rather than the customer outcome (order submissions succeeding), so it has to be de-tuned to avoid noise. Evidence: alerting review notes
[-] Why 4: Why is there no customer-outcome alert?
[-] No SLO-based alert on order-submit success exists; the service has an SLO but it is reported monthly, not alerted on. Evidence: SLO register entry for Order Capture
[-] Root cause RC-B: Alerting is based on infrastructure symptoms with de-tuned thresholds; there is no burn-rate alert on the customer-facing SLO.
[Heading2] 6.3 Chain C: the slow resolution (CH-3)
[-] Problem statement: It took 44 minutes from the first report to confirmed cause, 38 of them after the alert.
[-] Why?
[-] Because (answer, with evidence)
[-] Why 1: Why did diagnosis take 44 minutes?
[-] The first 18 minutes were spent on the Catalogue hypothesis, and pool saturation was only found by an ad-hoc query afterwards. Evidence: decision log 09:31 to 10:05
[-] Why 2: Why was pool saturation not seen sooner?
[-] Pool saturation is not on the service's dashboard, so responders did not see it. Evidence: dashboard 'Order Capture / Overview' panel list
[-] Why 3: Why is it not on the dashboard?
[-] The dashboard was generated from the standard service template, which has latency, errors and traffic panels but no saturation panels. Evidence: dashboard template v3 definition
[-] Why 4: Why does the template omit saturation?
[-] The template was never reviewed against the four golden signals guidance when it was adopted. Evidence: template adoption record, no review noted
[-] Root cause RC-C: The standard service dashboard template omits saturation signals, so responders lack the diagnostic metric for a whole class of failures.

[-] Lesson (what should have happened differently)

[-] Corrective action(s)
[-] LL-1
[-] We treated a change that quadrupled concurrency as low risk because it did not touch code. Capacity parameters are as risky as code and we should test and canary them the same way.

[-] CA-1, CA-4
[-] LL-2
[-] We found out from customer care, not from monitoring. We alert on what the infrastructure does, not on whether customers can place orders, and we have de-tuned those alerts to the point that they lag real impact by a quarter of an hour.

[-] CA-2
[-] LL-3
