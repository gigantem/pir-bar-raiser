[Heading1] Appendix A. Section guidance and worked example
[-] This appendix states the expectation for each section of the template and shows what good looks like. The examples all describe one fictional incident, rendered from the same packet the validator accepts, so that you can see how the sections connect: a lesson references a root cause, a corrective action references a lesson, and an action item references a corrective action.
[-] Worked example: Fictional incident used throughout: INC-2026-0412, Order Capture API: 11% of order submissions failed after a configuration change exhausted the database connection pool. The organisation, services, numbers and people are invented.
[Heading2] A.1 Summary
[-] Expectation. A single paragraph a reader with no context can understand in under a minute. It names the application, the customer-facing metric that moved, when, what happened in rough sequence, and the customer impact in numbers. It is written last, after every other section, because it summarises them. It contains no new facts that are not in the body. Pattern: <Application> experienced <metric> at <date time>. <What happened, rough timeline>. <Customer impact>.
[-] Common mistakes. Writing it first and never updating it. Describing the fix without the impact. Infrastructure language ("pods restarted") instead of customer language ("orders failed"). Vague quantities ("some customers", "a short period").
[-] Example: Order Capture API experienced an 11% HTTP 5xx error rate on order submissions from 09:12 to 10:26 AEST on 3 September 2026. Change CHG-88213, deployed at 09:04, raised the service's downstream concurrency limit from 50 to 200 without raising the database connection pool (fixed at 60), and the pool was exhausted under normal Wednesday-morning load. Customer care reported failures at 09:21, an alert fired at 09:27, pool exhaustion was confirmed as the cause at 10:05, and a rollback restored normal error rates at 10:26. Approximately 4,200 order submissions from around 3,100 customers failed during the window and 612 queued orders were delivered up to 40 minutes late. No orders were lost or duplicated. The rollback is a permanent fix for this change; the underlying gaps are addressed by CA-1 to CA-4.
[-] Key timings. Time to detect (TTD) 9 min; Time to know (TTK) 44 min; Time to remediate (TTR) 21 min; Customer impact duration 74 min; Time to resolve 95 min
[Heading2] A.2 Timeline
[-] Expectation. A distilled, timestamped sequence of the events that mattered: the precursor change, the first customer impact, the first signal, each hypothesis and the evidence that proved or rejected it, each decision, mitigation, resolution and the key communications. Every row has a timezone-qualified time and a source a reader could check. People appear by role, never by name. The phase column lets a reader see where the time went. Ten to thirty rows is typical for a Sev-1; summarise chat, do not paste it.
[-] Common mistakes. Starting at detection instead of impact start. Omitting rejected hypotheses, which hides time to know. "Team investigated" rows with no content.

[Heading2] A.4 Customer impact
[-] Expectation. Who was affected, what they experienced in their terms, how many, for how long, and which business metrics and commitments were hit. Say how each number was derived so a reviewer can trust it. Contact affected customers or partners to confirm impact; a PIR that reflects the customer's own account restores trust faster than one that guesses. State clearly what was not affected and whether any data was lost, duplicated or corrupted.
[-] Common mistakes. "Some customers were impacted.". Counting requests instead of customers. Omitting partners and internal consumers. Silence on data integrity, which readers will assume is bad news.
[-] Example: Who was affected (segments, regions, channels, partners)? Consumer and small-business customers submitting orders through the web and app channels in all regions.
[-] What did they experience? 'Something went wrong, please try again' on submit; retries also failed in most cases while the pool was exhausted.
[-] How many were affected? How do we know? 4,180 failed submissions from 3,090 distinct customer IDs between 09:12 and 10:26, about 11% of submissions in the window. Counted by: Log query PIR-0412-failed over the gateway access logs, de-duplicated by customer ID.
[-] For how long? 74 minutes of failed submissions (09:12 to 10:26), plus 21 minutes of delayed delivery for queued orders (to 10:47).
[-] Business metrics affected An estimated 1,900 orders were not re-attempted by 17:00 compared with the normal re-attempt rate, roughly $86k of order value at risk; 612 orders delivered 20 to 40 minutes late; support contacts 3.4 times the hourly norm between 09:30 and 11:00.
[-] Commitments breached (SLA, SLO, regulatory)? Monthly order-submit availability SLO (99.9%) consumed 58% of its error budget in one event. No contractual SLA breached.
[-] Customers / partners contacted? Two enterprise partners contacted by the account team at 11:30 on 3 Sep; both confirmed impact matched our estimate.
[-] What was not affected? Retail-store channel (separate order path); order status lookups; payments.
[-] Any data loss, duplication or corruption? No orders lost or duplicated, verified by reconciling the order queue against the orders table at 11:05.
[Heading2] A.5 Incident response and post-incident analysis
[-] Expectation. An honest account of what the response missed and how it can be fixed, broken down by detection, diagnosis, remediation, coordination and prevention. Each gap states its effect on the incident in time or impact, which is what makes corrective actions prioritisable. Record what went well and why, because those practices should be kept. This section identifies the distinct problems as channels of inquiry; the Five Whys explain them.
[-] Common mistakes. Only listing what went well. Gaps with no stated effect. Mixing causes with symptoms. Writing "human error" anywhere.
[-] Example: Went well: Once pool exhaustion was confirmed, the decision to roll back rather than tune was made in 9 minutes and executed in 12, because the rollback runbook was current and had been rehearsed in August.
[-] Went well: Customer care escalated the first reports to the on-call channel within 6 minutes of noticing them.
[-] Detection (time to detect): The only alert on this path requires 5% errors sustained for 10 minutes, so it fired 15 minutes after impact start and 6 minutes after customer care had already noticed. Effect: The first 9 minutes of the 74-minute impact window had no signal at all, and the next 6 relied on customer care rather than monitoring.
[-] Diagnosis (time to know): Pool saturation is not on the service dashboard; it was found by an ad-hoc query after the first hypothesis was rejected. Effect: Around 25 of the 44 minutes of time to know.
[-] Remediation (time to remediate): Rollback required a manual decision and a manual pipeline trigger; no automated rollback exists for this service. Effect: About 12 of the 21 minutes of time to remediate were mechanical steps a deploy guard could have taken.
[-] Prevention: CHG-88213 was classed as 'config only, low risk' and so required neither a load test nor a canary stage. Effect: The interaction between concurrency and pool size was never exercised before production.
[-] Channels of inquiry: CH-1: Why the configuration change exhausted the connection pool.; CH-2: Why the alert took 15 minutes and arrived after a customer-care report.; CH-3: Why diagnosis took 44 minutes from first report to confirmed cause.
[Heading2] A.6 Five Whys
[-] Expectation. One chain per channel of inquiry. Each chain starts from an observed fact and asks why until it reaches a cause that is a process, design or tooling gap rather than an action by an individual, and that would have prevented the problem regardless of who was on shift. Four to six whys is typical; the number is not magic. Each answer cites evidence from the timeline or metrics. Chains of kind failure, detection and resolution are required; a prior-actions chain is required for a repeat incident.
[-] Common mistakes. Stopping at "the engineer changed the config" (that is where the chain starts). A chain that reaches the right answer at why 2 and then pads to five. Answers with no evidence. One chain for everything.
[-] Example: Chain A, the failure (CH-1). Problem: POST /orders returned 5xx for 11% of requests from 09:12.
[-] Why 1: Why did requests fail? Requests timed out waiting for a database connection; pool in-use was 60 of 60 from 09:11. Evidence: FIG-2 panel A
[-] Why 2: Why was the pool exhausted? CHG-88213 raised downstream concurrency from 50 to 200, so up to 200 in-flight requests competed for 60 connections. Evidence: CHG-88213 diff; deploy log
[-] Why 3: Why could concurrency be raised without the pool? The two settings are tuned independently; nothing documents or enforces that pool size must scale with concurrency. Evidence: service configuration reference, no cross-reference between the two keys
[-] Why 4: Why was the interaction not caught before production? The change was not load-tested, so the interaction was never exercised. Evidence: CHG-88213 record shows no load-test stage
[-] Why 5: Why was it not load-tested? Our change policy classes configuration-only changes as low risk and exempts them from pre-production load testing and canary release. Evidence: change policy section 4.2
[-] RC-A: Change policy treats capacity-affecting configuration as low risk, so such changes reach production without load testing or a canary.
[-] Chain B, the detection gap (CH-2). Problem: The first alert fired 15 minutes after impact start, after a customer-care report.
[-] Why 1: Why did the alert take 15 minutes? The alert requires 5% errors sustained for 10 minutes. Evidence: alert A-2231 definition
[-] Why 2: Why is the window 10 minutes? It was widened from 2 to 10 minutes in May 2026 after false pages during a batch job. Evidence: alert change history, May 2026

[Heading2] A.7 Lessons learned
[-] Expectation. Numbered lessons, each derived from a labelled root cause, each stating what should have happened differently. Written in the first person plural and vocally self-critical: the team owns the gap. Lessons are about processes, tools and design, never people or named third parties. Every lesson maps to at least one corrective action.
[-] Common mistakes. Lessons that are really action items ("add an alert"). Lessons with no root cause reference. Softened language that nobody will act on ("could potentially consider").
[-] Example: LL-1 (RC-A): We treated a change that quadrupled concurrency as low risk because it did not touch code. Capacity parameters are as risky as code and we should test and canary them the same way. Corrective actions: CA-1, CA-4.
[-] LL-2 (RC-B): We found out from customer care, not from monitoring. We alert on what the infrastructure does, not on whether customers can place orders, and we have de-tuned those alerts to the point that they lag real impact by a quarter of an hour. Corrective actions: CA-2.
[-] LL-3 (RC-C): We spent 25 minutes looking for a metric that should have been on the first screen a responder opens. Our dashboard template does not show saturation, and we never checked it against our own guidance. Corrective actions: CA-3.
[Heading2] A.8 Corrective actions
[-] Expectation. High-level solutions that follow from the lessons, each tied to the lesson it addresses and each decomposed into action items. For every lesson ask the halving questions: what would halve the time to detect (health metrics, alarms)? the time to know (diagnostic metrics, triage tools)? the time to remediate (recovery tools, automation, automated rollback for deployment-related incidents)? Then ask what prevents recurrence altogether.
[-] Common mistakes. Corrective actions with no action items (they never happen). Only prevention and nothing for detection or recovery. Actions scoped to this team when the gap is shared.
[-] Example: CA-1 (LL-1, improves Prevention): Reclassify capacity-affecting configuration changes as performance-impacting so they require a load test and a canary stage. Action items AI-1, AI-2.
[-] CA-2 (LL-2, improves TTD): Alert on the order-submit success SLO using multi-window burn rate, so a real 11% failure pages within 2 minutes without adding noise. Action items AI-3, AI-4.
[-] CA-3 (LL-3, improves TTK): Add saturation panels (connection pools, thread pools, queue depth) to the standard dashboard template and roll out to all services that use it. Action items AI-5, AI-6.
[-] CA-4 (LL-1, improves TTR): Automatic rollback of a deployment when SLO burn rate crosses the fast-burn threshold within 30 minutes of the deploy. Action items AI-7.
[Heading2] A.9 Action items
[-] Expectation. Individual tasks that someone can do and someone else can verify as done, each traced to a corrective action, with an owner (a person or team who has agreed to it), a due date that is reasonable for the work rather than driven by the roadmap, a status, and a sentence on how it helps prevent recurrence. Be bold: if another team or a shared tool is the right place for the fix, say so and engage them. Never assign an action to someone who has not agreed. Five well-chosen items beat twenty.
[-] Common mistakes. "Investigate" or "Consider" items with no end state. Owners who were not asked. Due dates of "Q4". Items that address the symptom but would not stop the next occurrence.

[Heading1] Appendix B. PIR principles, timing, presenting and reviewing
[-] Condensed from the reference guidance "What Good Looks Like for Post-Incident Reviews". Keep this appendix in the template; remove it from a published PIR if your readers do not need it.
[Heading2] B.1 What a PIR is and who it is for
[ListBullet] A PIR helps you understand why an outage, error or miss occurred, and ensures corrective mechanisms are in place to prevent recurrence regardless of operator.
[ListBullet] The focus is on fixing issues and improving processes, not assigning blame. PIRs are about processes, not people. Never blame or name individuals.
[ListBullet] An incident is a short cut straight to a real problem that, when fixed, will improve the current state. Never waste the opportunity.
[ListBullet] It is written for your team, your customers, your partners, your dependencies, your stakeholders, and yourself.
[Heading2] B.2 Timing
[ListBullet] Start once customer impact is mitigated.
[ListBullet] Draft within 2 to 3 business days: capture details while they are fresh and while high-resolution metrics are still available.
[ListBullet] Publish within 1 to 2 weeks: reach consensus and define a plan.
[ListBullet] Do not delay because the root cause is incomplete; begin anyway. It is acceptable for final completion to take longer, especially where third parties are involved.
[Heading2] B.3 Ownership and writing order
[ListBullet] The Product Owner owns the PIR and presents it, drawing on the Incident Manager and subject-matter experts.
[ListBullet] Write in this order: Timeline, Metrics and graphs, Customer impact, Incident response analysis, Five Whys, Lessons learned, Corrective actions, Action items, and finally Summary.
[ListBullet] Consider the PIR's impact on the roadmap, but do not let the roadmap drive the dates.
[Heading2] B.4 The chain of traceability
[ListBullet] Each Five Whys chain ends in a labelled root cause.
[ListBullet] Each lesson learned is numbered and references the root cause it derives from.
[ListBullet] Each lesson has at least one corrective action: a high-level solution to the issue.
[ListBullet] Each corrective action has at least one action item: an individual task with an owner and a reasonable due date.
[ListBullet] For every action item, ask whether it will prevent a re-occurrence of the event. Never blindly assign action items.
[Heading2] B.5 Presenting a PIR
[ListBullet] No slide deck. Work through the PIR document directly.
[ListBullet] Bring the subject-matter experts. The Product Owner presents.
[ListBullet] No customer or stakeholder surprises: align beforehand.
[ListBullet] Assign a dedicated note-taker.
[ListBullet] Present facts and be data-specific. Be open, not defensive. Be prepared to say "I don't know, I will get back to you all with an answer."
[Heading2] B.6 Checklist for reviewers
[ListBullet] Avoid blame.
[ListBullet] Focus on the timeline, the impact and metrics, the Five Whys, and the solutions.
[ListBullet] Do not nitpick grammar or writing style.
[ListBullet] Confirm whether the action items can prevent repeat issues.
[ListBullet] Are due dates in place?
[Heading2] B.7 Where to get help
[ListBullet] SRE Champions.
[ListBullet] Centres of Excellence (COEs).
[ListBullet] A PIR mailing list or community channel [insert your organisation's link].
